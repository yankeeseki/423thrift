🤝 How the Chattanooga Thrift Trail Helps Local Shops, Vendors and Charities

Community November 13, 2026 · 5 min read

One route through Chattanooga’s thrift, vintage and antique shops reaches a lot more than a shopping bag’s worth of finds. Here is what is on the Chattanooga Thrift Trail, by the numbers:

  • 49 shops on one map
  • 23 locally owned small businesses
  • 800+ local vendors with booths inside seven of those shops
  • 17 charities, funded by 25 Trail shops
  • Thousands of children, families, neighbors and pets helped by those charities

Every route you plan sends shoppers to all of them. Here are the numbers behind each one, with sources.

Local vendors: more than 800 booths on the Trail

Most of the Trail’s vintage and antique shops are not run by one owner. They are made up of many small sellers, each running a booth as a tiny business of their own (see What It Costs to Be a Vendor). When a shop gets busier, every one of those vendors gets more chances to sell. Here are the most recent counts we found for each shop, and where each one came from:

  • Mercantile at the Ridge (East Ridge): over 300 vendors in a 60,000-square-foot building (Times Free Press, 2021). It opened in August 2018 as Chattanooga Mercantile, in the old East Ridge Flea Market building (Times Free Press, 2019).
  • Vinterest (Hixson and Southside): over 180 vendors. That is over 100 in Hixson (Times Free Press, 2018) plus over 80 at the Southside store (Vinterest Southside, 2026). Across all of its stores, including Nashville, CityScope reports over 250 vendors.
  • Dirty Jane’s Antiques (Red Bank): over 100 vendors in a 24,000-square-foot warehouse (CityScope).
  • Crafter’s Emporium & Book Exchange (near Hixson): over 90 small businesses under one roof (NOOGAtoday).
  • East Ridge Antique District: over 65 vendors in just one of its shops, Department One Three (its Yellow Pages listing). The district has eight antique shops across more than 70,000 square feet (Times Free Press, 2021), so the full count is higher.
  • The Refindery on McCallie (Highland Park): about 50 vendors in 16,000 square feet, according to its owner (Times Free Press, 2023).
  • Electric Crocodile (North Shore): over 20 vendors (NOOGAtoday).

Add those up and you get more than 800 vendors at seven stops: 300 + 180 + 100 + 90 + 65 + 50 + 20. Some of those counts are a few years old, and shops tend to grow, not shrink, so we use the newest figure we found for each. The Trail also lists more multi-vendor and curated shops, such as Parker’s Picks and Vendor Mall, Edith’s Emporium and Vintage & More On The Blvd, that are not in the total.

Local small businesses: 23 locally owned shops

Of the Trail’s 49 shops, 23 are independent, locally owned vintage, antique and resale shops. The shops span East Ridge, Brainerd, Red Bank, Hixson, the North Shore, Southside, Fort Oglethorpe and more. A route that links them puts the shops on a shopper’s plan before they ever leave the house. A customer who set out for one shop plans a stop at three more. Add a coffee stop or lunch to your route with Add Your Own Stop, and the cafés and restaurants between the shops get the visit, too.

Local charities: shops that fund the work

Twenty-five Trail shops send their proceeds to 17 different charities: Adult & Teen Challenge MidSouth, Bachman Community Center, Bethel Bible Village, Chambliss Center for Children, Chattanooga Rescue Mission, Chattanooga Room in the Inn, Goodwill Industries, Habitat for Humanity, Humane Educational Society, Ladies of Charity, Northside Neighborhood House, Providence Ministries, Samaritan Center, Signal Mountain Social Services, St. Timothy’s, the Sexual Assault Victims Advocacy Center and The Chattery. For many of them the thrift shop is a large share of the budget. The latest numbers each one has reported:

  • Northside Neighborhood House says its thrift stores provide about half its funding, and it assisted 4,000 people in 2013 (Times Free Press, 2014). Its tax filings report the thrift stores served 104,800 customers and that it helped 604 households with rent, mortgage or utility bills in the year ending June 2024 (Cause IQ, Intellispect).
  • Chambliss Center for Children runs two thrift stores whose revenue helps serve more than 750 children (Chattanooga Trend). Its 2023 numbers: 749 children in childcare, 49 in foster care and 34 young adults in its Transitional Living Program.
  • Habitat for Humanity of Greater Chattanooga has built more than 300 homes in 38 years (WDEF). Its ReStore proceeds support that work.
  • Chattanooga Room in the Inn has helped more than 3,000 homeless women and children since it began in 1988 (Chattanoogan, 2009).
  • Humane Educational Society takes in four to five thousand homeless pets each year, finds homes for over 3,500 animals and sees nearly 7,000 clinic patients (heschatt.org).
  • Bachman Community Center says its volunteer-staffed thrift store provides almost 50% of its operating budget (bachmancc.org).

Every purchase at those shops goes to work in Chattanooga, and every route that includes one is another reason to walk in. See Charity Thrift Stores in Chattanooga: Where Your Money Goes for more.

One route, three kinds of local benefit

  • Vendors: more than 800 booths at seven shops.
  • Small businesses: 23 locally owned shops on one map.
  • Charities: 17 groups serving thousands of children, families, neighbors and pets.

Plan your next thrift day around a stop from each.
